A distributed system is a collection of independent computers that appears to its users as a single coherent system. The computers do not share memory or a clock; they cooperate by passing messages over a network that can delay, reorder, duplicate or lose them. Because any machine can stop at any moment, and because a slow machine cannot be told apart from a stopped one, every design in this field is a decision about what to give up when things go wrong. Replication keeps several copies of the same data so that a failure does not lose it, but then the copies must be kept in agreement, and agreement costs round trips. Partitioning spreads the data over many machines so that no single one has to hold all of it, but then a request that touches two partitions needs coordination, and coordination costs round trips as well. Caching hides latency until the cached value is stale. Timeouts turn silence into a decision, and the decision is sometimes wrong. Consensus protocols such as Paxos and Raft let a majority of machines agree on a sequence of values even if a minority fails, at the price of waiting for that majority on every write. Clocks drift, so the order of two events on different machines is known only when a message connects them; logical clocks and vector clocks record exactly that and nothing more. Idempotent operations make retries safe, and retries are how a system survives a lost reply. Backpressure keeps a fast sender from burying a slow receiver. Observability is the practice of making the system explain itself, because nobody can attach a debugger to forty machines at once. When a single large computation is split across machines, the cost that matters is rarely the bandwidth: it is how many times per unit of work one machine has to wait for another. A pipeline that hands its result to the next stage once per item pays one network delay per stage; a star in which a central node asks each worker in turn, and waits for each answer, pays two delays per worker for every step, and often several steps per item.
